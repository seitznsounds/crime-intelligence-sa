Abstract
Managing the dynamics between corruption and human rights law is part of the
everyday work of asset recovery practitioners. While acts of corruption may diminish the
human rights and freedoms of citizens, asset recovery measures may also restrict the
rights of alleged corrupt officials and third parties. These tensions cannot be resolved in
the abstract. Instead, they must be moderated by particular people in particular matters
with reference to particular asset recovery and human rights norms. To build that
specialized knowledge base, the Stolen Asset Recovery Initiative (StAR) here presents
a review of almost 200 cases on confiscation and international cooperation from the
European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR). A busy and influential international human
rights tribunal, the ECtHR’s jurisprudence is rich with insights on how human rights could
apply in asset recovery contexts. Its cases have direct relevance to practitioners working
with or in important European financial centers and serve as a reference point for others.
Summarizing the key ECtHR judgments and synthesizing them with recent jurispru-
dence, this publication aims to help practitioners secure robust outcomes by illustrating
how asset recovery processes interact with human rights laws.

The dynamics between corruption and human rights law matter to asset recovery
practitioners and the effectiveness of their efforts to return illicit wealth to countries of
origin. Whereas human rights can provide an important motivation for that painstaking
and sometimes challenging work, they can also derail or delegitimate those operations.
Even in the simplest international asset recovery case, human rights risks may manifest
in at least three ways. First, within the country of origin, superior courts may quash the
criminal convictions, the (NCB) confiscation orders, or both if the affected parties were
denied basic rights. Second, if those appeals fail, the destination country may refuse
to enforce the confiscation order because the originating proceedings do not accord
with its basic norms or applicable human rights laws. Third, the respondent party to the
confiscation order may attack the criminal, confiscation, or cooperation proceedings
in an international human rights tribunal.9 A victory there may result in an order to pay
compensation, as well as negative publicity for the cooperating states and their wider
anticorruption and asset recovery efforts.

EXECUTIvE SUMMARY
Human rights thus matter greatly to the Stolen Asset Recovery Initiative (StAR), which
is mandated to help countries deny safe haven for corrupt funds and facilitate the return
of illicit wealth. A collaboration between the World Bank and the UN Office on Drugs and
Crime (UNODC), StAR supports affected jurisdictions, generates research, and contrib-
utes to international debates on anticorruption and asset recovery. As early as 2011,
StAR had called for informed dialogue around human rights issues in international asset
recovery processes involving extradition and mutual legal assistance (MLA).10 It encour-
aged destination countries to “closely examine the circumstances of each case before
determining, on specific and articulable grounds, that due process was not provided.…”11
Soon after that, StAR advocated for a human rights-based approach to illicit enrichment,
which is suggested for criminalization under the UN Convention Against Corruption
(UNCAC).12 More recently still, StAR has described how member countries can use
human rights frameworks to achieve “payback” for victims of corruption,13 echoing
messages from other bodies within the UN system.14
To date, StAR has not analyzed how specific human rights laws apply to the sorts of
confiscation and cooperation processes that are envisaged by UNCAC.15 Recognizing
that gap, StAR reviewed almost 200 cases on confiscation and international cooperation
from the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) between August 2021 and Decem-
ber 2024. Sitting in Strasbourg, France, the ECtHR is a regional human rights tribunal
that decides disputes between state parties and people under the Council of Europe’s
Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms (ECHR) and
its protocols (ECHR-P1, and so on).16
While ECtHR judgments and decisions are only binding on their parties, they are
highly suggestive as to how the ECtHR may decide future cases and highly influential
in the wider international human rights system, in which confiscation and cooperation
are largely untested.17 The Court's influence comes firstly through its ability to provide
persuasive precedents for Council of Europe states when interpreting domestic con-
stitutions and fundamental right laws. In addition, the ECtHR is a norm-shaper among
international tribunals that decide human rights disputes in other regions (e.g., in the
Americas and Africa). The ECtHR's jurisprudence on human rights thereby helps shape
domestic and international understandings of civil and political liberties, democracy
and the rule of law. Furthermore, the ECHR and protocols are important to countries
cooperating with ECHR state parties, who have limited obligations to assess the quality
of originating processes according to the ECtHR.18
Summarizing the key ECtHR judgements and synthesizing the latest decisions, this
publication presents a jurisprudence that is rich with information on the application
of human rights to confiscation and cooperation procedures. The ECtHR has decided
many confiscation and cooperation matters, including those involving prominent
financial centers, politically exposed persons, and crackdowns on corruption and
organized crime. The cases are often highly technical, their outcomes depending on
differences in the wording between articles of the ECHR and the wording and context of
the relevant confiscation and cooperation laws in respondent states. There are, however,
cross-cutting themes around the importance of observing legality in the drafting and
application of confiscation and cooperation laws, the proportionality of the coercive
measure and its restrictions on human freedom, and the presence and adequacy of
procedural safeguards for affected parties. Purpose is also relevant to the degree of
protection afforded by the ECtHR’s due process and property guarantees, as the ECtHR
often uses the context of a confiscation law to determine what a confiscation order was
supposed to accomplish.
This publication presents these headlines and details in four chapters. The first
two chapters are about the right to a fair trial as elaborated in Art. 6 ECHR. For
example, paragraph 1 of Art. 6 applies to proceedings that determine a person’s “civil
rights and obligations” or “criminal charge”; paragraphs 2 and 3 apply to proceedings
in which a person is “charged” with an offense. Chapter 1 finds that the civil limb of

Art. 6(1) covers non-punitive domestic confiscation orders, and that the criminal limb
captures domestic confiscation orders that punish. However, the latter generally do
not “charge” an offender with a new offense and thus do not trigger the more rigorous
rights to a presumption of innocence and a defense under Art. 6(2)–(3) ECHR. The
general right to a fair trial under Art. 6(1) ECHR permits presumptions of fact and law
subject to safeguards and an independent evaluation of the evidence in a particular
case. Third-party confiscation is compatible with Art. 6(1) ECHR subject to sufficient
safeguards.
Chapter 2 extends that analysis of Art. 6 ECHR to cooperative confiscation orders.
These are confiscation orders that are issued in response to a request for assistance
from another country or an international organization. After describing the mechanisms
by which cooperative orders are issued under the UNCAC, the chapter reviews the
ECtHR case law on those orders and other acts of cooperation. It finds that requested
ECHR state parties must ensure that they determine the enforceability of foreign
confiscation orders in a way that is fair under Art. 6(1) ECHR, civil limb. In addition, when
states directly enforce confiscation orders from a state that is not party to the ECHR, it
is likely that they should at least ensure that the foreign proceedings were not a “flagrant
denial of justice.” Different tests apply when confiscation is ordered by the UN Security
Council or under European Union regulations.
Chapter 3 presents the findings of the analysis under the freedom from ex post facto
criminal punishments (Art. 7 ECHR). Paragraph 1 of Art. 7 prohibits the retroactive appli-
cation of offenses and penalties to the detriment of an accused.19 “[I]t also embodies,
more generally, the principle that only the law can define a crime and prescribe a penalty
(nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege) and the principle that the criminal law must not be
extensively construed to an accused’s detriment, for instance by analogy.”20 The chapter
finds that punitive confiscation orders are a type of penalty under Art. 7 ECHR and so
may only be imposed prospectively. Such orders should also be limited, in their personal
scope, to an offender who has been found liable for an offense or third parties who were
allowed to participate in the criminal proceedings. Orders to enforce foreign confiscation
orders are generally not punitive, at least when the foreign orders were foreseeable in the
requested country.
Finally, chapter 4 is about the right to property (Art. 1 ECHR-P1), as well as ne bis in
idem (Art. 4(1) ECHR-P7), discrimination (Art. 14 ECHR), and abuses of process (Art.
18 ECHR). That final chapter reports that the ECtHR has adopted a broad concept of
property under Art. 1 ECHR-P1, with the consequence that it has considered many
confiscation orders as affecting protected “possessions.” The key question under
Art. 1 ECHR-P1 is thus generally whether confiscation orders are lawful and justified
interferences with property. States have a wide margin of appreciation to protect
the community through confiscation and related acts of cooperation under Art. 1
ECHR-P1. Nonetheless, confiscation orders must be lawful and must not impose an
“individual and excessive burden” on an applicant, given their procedure, scope, and
context. Article 4(1) ECHR-P7 and Arts. 14 and 18 ECHR have a much narrower scope
but are also relevant given the targeted nature of some asset recovery laws and the
political context of many corruption matters.
Overall, the publication shows that there is much to be learned from the ECtHR’s
case law on confiscation and cooperation and that those lessons are a starting point
for further work by practitioners and organizations like StAR. For practitioners, the
publication shows that the ECtHR is a thought leader on the topic of human rights and
asset recovery under international law. The international legal system, however, is only
one system in which those tensions play out, and the Strasbourg court is only one
international (regional) tribunal. This summary of its jurisprudence is extensive, but it is
just that. Practitioners will need to analyze the human rights issues in particular cases,
considering the jurisdictions relevant to such matters and the advice of lawyers in those
places. For international organizations, this publication indicates the need for more

The structure of Article 6
Article 6 ECHR is a set of rights to fair treatment in civil and criminal proceedings. It
distinguishes between proceedings that “determin[e] … civil rights and obligations or
… any criminal charge” and proceedings in which a person is “charged with a criminal
offence.” The ECtHR has defined a “criminal charge” as an “official notification given
to an individual by the competent authority of an allegation that he has committed a
criminal offence. …”1 The “criminal” nature of the offense is gauged having regard to the
three “Engel” criteria:2 (a) the designation of the offense in domestic law, (b) the nature of
the alleged misconduct, and (c) the severity of the potential penalty.3 The concept of “civil
rights and obligations” includes some disputes between government authorities and
private persons that are “decisive for private rights and obligations.”4
Varieties in domestic confiscation procedures
Therefore, a key question is how domestic confiscation orders are classified under Art.
6 ECHR. The issue is complicated by the variety of confiscation procedures in domestic
law, as reflected in the international anticorruption and money laundering instruments
like the United Nations Convention Against Corruption (UNCAC).

Confiscating authority
First, Art. 31(1) UNCAC does not specify which branch of government should order

confiscation to include orders by “court[s] or other competent authorit[ies].”7 There are,

Matters of proof
Third, the UNCAC foresees diversity in how states establish the commission of an
offense and the link between a thing and an offense. It permits but does not require
these facts to be established in a criminal trial according to a criminal standard of proof.
It says that states should consider confiscation without conviction if “the offender can-
not be prosecuted by reason of death, flight or absence or in other appropriate cases.”12
It also encourages the use of presumptions, which are procedural devices that help the
state prove the illicit conduct or connection of the assets.13
This diversity is reflective of the variety in domestic confiscation practice:
• Conviction-based (or criminal) confiscation procedures have the most onerous
requirements. The state convicts the offender, thereby proving the triggering offense
to the criminal standard,14 and it proves the connection between the specific offense
and the things that are liable to confiscation.15
• Non-conviction-based (NCB) confiscation laws are like “traditional” criminal
confiscation laws in that they require the state to prove that property is connected to
criminal activity,16 but they use a lower standard of proof for that conduct than what
is typically used in a criminal trial.17 The offender is not formally charged with an
offense, nor is there a determination of their guilt or innocence.18 NCB schemes may
“for[m] part of the criminal confiscation regime” or be independent from it.19
• Extended confiscation powers blend conviction and NCB confiscation procedures.
These powers are triggered by a conviction, yet they do not require a state to prove all
of the offenses to which the property relates to the criminal standard.20 Instead, when
an offender is convicted of a designated offense, the law permits the confiscation of
other criminal proceeds.21 Other markers (such as evidence of a criminal “lifestyle”)
are used to support a conclusion (or a presumption) that assets are connected to
crime.22
• Rebuttable presumptions (or reversed burdens of proof) concern the illicit nature
of the wealth or the commission of other (serious) offenses.23 They allow the confis-
cating authority to infer that the wealth is relevantly connected to an offense if the
affected party fails to “presen[t] proof to rebut the presumption.”24 In confiscation
matters, the standard of proof for establishing (and rebutting) the presumption is
generally below the criminal standard.25

The finding in Phillips
The applicant, Phillips, had been convicted of drug trafficking and made the subject of
a confiscation order under sec. 2 Drug Trafficking Act of 1994 (UK).27 The Crown Court
had calculated the amount of Phillips’s benefit from crime by presuming that he had
engaged in other acts of criminality.28 The presumption could have been rebutted on the
balance of probabilities but was not in Phillips’s case. A total order of £91,400 was made
against him,29 and it was to be paid in three years or supplemented by two years in jail in
case of default.30
Phillips therefore argued that the confiscation proceedings had “charged” him with
new offenses and therefore triggered the requirement for a presumption of innocence
under Art. 6(2) ECHR. The ECtHR responded to that argument by adapting and applying
the three Engel criteria, “namely, the classification of the proceedings under national law,
their essential nature and the type and severity of the penalty that the applicant risked
incurring… .”31 The court noted that the confiscation orders were considered part of the
post-conviction sentencing process in English law.32 The purpose of the confiscation
procedure was to assist in the calculation of the confiscation order, not to determine the
applicant’s liability to further offenses of drug trafficking, such as would be “reflected
in his criminal record.”33 On this basis, the ECtHR concluded that Art. 6(2) ECHR was
inapplicable.
Instead, the Strasbourg judges likened the confiscation proceedings to a sentencing
procedure that was subject to Art. 6(1) ECHR.34 The court reasoned that “Article 6 § 1
applies throughout the entirety of proceedings for ‘the determination of … any criminal
charge’, including proceedings whereby a sentence is fixed.”35 The right to be presumed
innocent (and have the prosecution discharge the burden of proof) was part of the
“general notion of a fair hearing” in Art. 6(1).36 It was not, however, absolute.37
[P]resumptions of fact or of law operate in every criminal-law system and are not
prohibited in principle by the Convention, as long as States remain within certain
limits, taking into account the importance of what is at stake and maintaining the
rights of the defence … .38
The presumption of illicit acquisition increased the severity of the sanction rather than
pronounced Phillips’s guilt. It did not therefore “charge” him with a new offense.39 This
was so even though the English judges were required to assume past criminal conduct
to calculate benefit.40 Overall, the presumption was compatible with Phillips’s right to a
fair hearing in Art. 6(1) ECHR because41
• The proceeding was subject to the safeguards of a “judicial proceeding” such as the
provision of a “public hearing, advance disclosure of the prosecution case and the
opportunity for the applicant to adduce documentary and oral evidence”;
• The applicant was given an opportunity to rebut the presumption with evidence about
the origins of his wealth, the amount of his available assets, or both; and
• The domestic courts did not rely only on the presumption but used the available
evidence to determine that specific assets had an illicit source.

Article 6 and non-conviction-based confiscation
procedures
The ECtHR has also considered various types of NCB confiscation orders under Art. 6
ECHR. Some of these orders have removed the proceeds of drug trafficking, organized
criminality, and corruption,45 whereas others have targeted instrumentalities or objects
of offenses like smuggling.46 Still others have removed items that were seized as
evidence and confiscated due to an unspecified connection with criminal matters.47
Across these cases, t he ECtHR has applied the civil limb of Art. 6(1) ECHR. The court
has reasoned that the NCB confiscation proceedings were “civil” because they deter-
mined “pecuniary”48 or “property” rights.49 They were not “criminal” because they were
not designated as such in domestic law and did not operate as such, given their nature
and severity:
• Designation: The ECtHR has noted that NCB confiscation proceedings are some-
times deemed in rem, “civil” (or non-criminal), or both, in the domestic law of the
confiscating state.50
• Nature: The court has often determined that NCB confiscation proceedings have
preventive or compensatory rather than punitive goals in domestic confiscation law.51
Alternatively or in addition, the court has considered it important that the proceedings
did not pronounce the guilt of the applicant,52 depend on the applicant’s conviction,53
or affect their criminal record.54 When NCB confiscation orders have followed an
acquittal, it was material that they were “separate and distinct in timing, procedure
and content” from the original criminal proceedings and not based on the crimes that
were found to have no basis.55
• Severity: The size of NCB confiscation orders has not been determinative of their
characterization, even when they are “substantial” or “hefty.”56
To say that proceedings are civil is not to say that they are outside the scope of Art. 6. To
the contrary, the civil limb of Art. 6(1) ECHR also entails rights to a fair hearing, access to
a court, and determinations within reasonable time. Those rights, however, are qualified,57
and state parties have a margin of appreciation in both the design and execution of “civil”
procedures and associated rules of evidence.58 In one NCB confiscation case, the ECtHR
asked whether the respondent state’s procedures
afforded the applicant, in the light of the severity of the measure to which she was
liable, an adequate opportunity to put her case to the courts, pleading, as the case

or through others who were entitled to challenge the confiscation application.64 In
addition, the applicant(s) may waive their entitlements to participate in the relevant
process.65
• A public, oral hearing at first instance: This must be afforded to an applicant, at
least where the parties disagreed about matters of fact (such as origins or ownership
of assets) or credibility of witnesses.66 It may be permissible for appeal proceedings
to be conducted in camera and using the parties’ written submission if the applicants
had a prior opportunity for a public, oral hearing in which they were represented by
their lawyers.67
• Rights to a defense: The applicant must have benefited from the rights of the
defense, such as legal representation of their choosing and rights to participate in the
process through the raising of objections and production of evidence;68 the list in Art.
6(3) ECHR is indicative.
• No arbitrary decision-making: The domestic court’s conclusions about the illicit
origins of the wealth must not have been arbitrary.69 The confiscating judge must
have critically engaged with the evidence from both sides about whether wealth was
licit or illicit, and provided a reasoned judgment as to why they concluded that the
assets were liable to confiscation.70 It is not sufficient for a court simply to cite the
prosecutor’s case.71
• Reasonable time: Article 6(1) ECHR requires the determination of civil proceedings in
a reasonable time. Confiscation proceedings may violate Art. 6(1) (civil limb) because
they were unreasonably long.72

It follows from cases like Silickienè, that ECHR state parties do not automatically violate

the opportunity for applicants to contest confiscation orders before domestic courts in
adversarial processes.74 When states have not formally enshrined the requisite proce-

Box 1.2. What about posthumous confiscation orders? Silickienè v. Lithuania
Article 54(1)(c) UNCAC requires state parties to consider allowing confiscation without conviction when
offenders cannot be prosecuted, inter alia, because of (their) death. In such cases, the confiscation order
will often affect third parties, such as co-owners of assets or beneficiaries under a will.
The case of Silickienè v. Lithuania dealt with the question of whether confiscation in such cases is
compatible with the right to a fair trial and, in particular, the presumption of innocence in Art. 6(2) ECHR.a
The applicant’s husband had been a “high-ranking tax police officer” who was charged inter alia with
leading a smuggling operation.b He died on remand.c After his death, the prosecutor discontinued the
criminal case against him, though it secured convictions against three co-accused and a confiscation
order affecting the applicant and her mother-in-law.d
Under Art. 6(2) ECHR, the applicant alleged that confiscation of her property was based on a posthu-
mous finding of guilt against her husband, which she was forced to inherit.e The ECtHR confirmed that
criminal liability is personal and that it would carefully scrutinize the imposition of liability “on the living
in respect of acts apparently committed by the deceased.”f A person could be also “charged” with an
offense outside the official criminal process.g
There was nonetheless no application and no violation of Art. 6(2) ECHR because this confiscation
order related to the proceeds of the “entire criminal organisation” in which the husband had been a
member and in relation to acts for which three other persons had been convicted.h The funds had been
connected, through evidence in the confiscation proceeding, to the criminal organization, its (living)
members, and its operations.i The ECtHR also distinguished the applicant’s case from previous matters
on the basis that the proceedings against all the accused continued after the death of the husband,
rather than being initiated afresh after his passing.j Consequently, the applicant neither inherited her

Cooperative confiscation procedures
International cooperation is necessary when legislative, judicial, or enforcement juris-
diction is split between states. In confiscation cases, illicit wealth may be held in one
jurisdiction, while the offense may have been committed in another.
Articles 54 and 55 UNCAC provide for this eventuality. Article 54(1) UNCAC contains
duties to (a) permit authorities to give effect to foreign confiscation orders or (b) conduct
new local criminal or other proceedings to confiscate the foreign illicit wealth. Article
55(1) UNCAC outlines the corresponding duty to assist in cooperative confiscation
cases by obtaining a new local confiscation order from their competent authorities, and
“if such an order is granted,” carry it out (indirect enforcement) or by submitting a foreign
confiscation order to a locally competent authority “with a view to giving [it] effect”
directly in domestic law (direct enforcement). Article 55 UNCAC assumes and extends
the UNCAC’s rules on the refusal of MLA requests in Art. 46.1
The rules the UNCAC established on the refusal of requests are extensive but not
comprehensive. On the one hand, they cede to other MLA treaties between the parties.2
The parties are encouraged, but not required, to rely on the UNCAC’s provisions, even if
the other agreement provides less extensive coverages of MLA matters.3 On the other
hand, the UNCAC does not expressly permit state parties to refuse to assist because of
the non-compliance of domestic criminal or confiscation proceedings with international
human rights laws.4 Consequently, it also says nothing about the power of state parties
to seek assurances that those rights will be respected in the MLA process.
The scope of the UNCAC’s cooperative confiscation obligations and the gap in the
grounds for refusal create issues from a human rights perspective. The issues are both
how such cooperative confiscation proceedings should be characterized under the
civil-criminal dichotomy of Art. 6 ECHR, and when requested ECHR state parties must
refuse assistance over concerns with the quality of the foreign proceedings on which
the new local orders are based. These issues are most problematic when state parties
directly enforce foreign confiscation orders and are considered in that connection.
Cooperative confiscation procedures affect civil rights
and obligations
The leading case on cooperative confiscation is Saccoccia v. Austria.5 Saccoccia stands
for the proposition that proceedings to enforce a foreign confiscation order are civil
matters under Art. 6(1) ECHR, and that the right to a public oral hearing in that paragraph
may be limited by the nature of the enforcement action.
The applicant, Saccoccia, had been convicted and sentenced in the United States
for laundering the proceeds of drug trafficking.6 The US courts also issued a forfeiture
order.7 The US forfeiture order covered US$136 million in proceeds and substitute
assets, including cash, bonds, and other financial instruments found in the applicant’s
Austrian rental apartment.8 Following a request from the United States, Austrian courts
had conducted exequatur (enforcement) proceedings and ordered the confiscation of
the assets to give effect to the US forfeiture order.9
Before the ECtHR, the applicant complained that Austria had violated his rights to
due process and property. Under Art. 6 ECHR, he claimed that both the criminal and civil
parts of Art. 6 ECHR were activated.10 He then alleged that the Austrian judges had paid

of the dispute was decisive for whether or not the applicant could exercise his rights as

Applying the civil limb of Art. 6(1) ECHR, the ECtHR then considered whether Austria
had provided an appropriate degree of protection to the applicant. The enforcement
proceedings had not been held in public, and the applicant had not been provided with
a chance to appear personally.15 Finding for the state, the ECtHR confirmed that the
“nature of the issues” would decide the applicability of the public oral hearing require-

technical issues of inter-State cooperation in combating money-laundering through the

“limited” in nature.18 They did not require the Austrian court to review the merits of the US
forfeiture order, assess the credibility of the applicant, or hear witnesses.19
In these circumstances, the courts could fairly and reasonably decide the case on
the basis of the parties’ written submissions and other written materials. They were
therefore dispensed from holding a hearing.20
The standard for reviewing the proceedings in the
requesting state
Soccoccia thus was decided on the strength of the safeguards for the applicant in the
requested state (Austria),21 but as previously noted, ECHR state parties sometimes have
duties to protect persons from violations of convention rights and freedoms that could
occur abroad. The convention does not apply outside its territory in these cases22 and it
does not bind non-contracting countries.23 Instead, ECHR state parties have duties to be
diligent (careful) in protecting people from violations of convention rights and freedoms
through their own conduct in international cooperation, expulsion, and rendition.24
The “flagrant denial of justice” test in extradition cases
The key case on Art. 6 in international cooperation is Soering v. United Kingdom. The
applicant was to be extradited from the United Kingdom to the United States to face
charges of capital murder.25 He alleged that extradition would expose him to “death
row phenomenon,” a psychological condition caused by waiting for execution that was
equivalent to “torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment” under Art.
3 ECHR.26 He also claimed that he was unlikely to receive a fair trial in the United States
under Art. 6 ECHR due to the lack of legal aid in the relevant court system and his own
likely inability to fund legal challenges.27
The ECtHR agreed that the decision to extradite an applicant could engage the United
Kingdom’s responsibility under Art. 3 ECHR.28 The test was whether “substantial grounds

have been shown for believing that the person concerned, if extradited, faces a real risk
of being subjected to torture or to inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment in the

By contrast, there was no violation of Art. 6. The ECtHR “[did] not exclude that an

The “flagrant denial of justice” in expulsion cases
The ECtHR elaborated and applied the concept of a flagrant denial of justice in Othman
(Abu Qatada) v. United Kingdom. That case was about the expulsion of a Jordanian
national who had been resident in the United Kingdom as an asylum seeker.32
While waiting for indefinite leave to remain in the United Kingdom,33 Othman was
convicted in absentia of terrorism offenses in Jordan and sentenced to 15 years
imprisonment with hard labor.34 British authorities initially considered that Othman
could not be returned to Jordan due to the possibility of ill treatment under Art. 3 ECHR.35
Subsequently, the United Kingdom brokered a memorandum of understanding (MOU)
with Jordan on the return of persons like Othman. That MOU contained assurances
about humane treatment that were to be monitored by a Jordanian non-governmental
organization (NGO).36 The day after the MOU was signed, the United Kingdom served the
applicant with a deportation notice.37
The applicant challenged the deportation notice before the UK courts and ultimately
the ECtHR, alleging that there was a real risk he would be tortured or otherwise ill-treated
contrary to Art. 3 ECHR in Jordan.38 That claim was unsuccessful. The ECtHR also dis-
missed the applicant’s complaints under Art. 13 ECHR (regarding the right to a remedy in
the United Kingdom) and Art. 5 (regarding alleged unlawful detention in Jordan).39
By contrast, the court considered there was a real risk that the applicant would be
retried on the basis of testimonies obtained through torture, contrary to Art. 6 ECHR.40
Elaborating the obiter in Soering, the ECtHR defined “flagrant denial of justice” (at
para. 260) as “a breach of the principles of fair trial guaranteed by Article 6 which is so
fundamental as to amount to a nullification, or destruction of the very essence, of the
right guaranteed by that Article.” The admission of torture evidence would meet that
standard,41 as could the following circumstances:
• conviction in absentia with no possibility subsequently to obtain a fresh determination
of the merits of the charge … ;
• a trial which is summary in nature and conducted with a total disregard for the rights
of the defence … ;
• detention without any access to an independent and impartial tribunal to have the
legality [of] the detention reviewed … ;
• deliberate and systematic refusal of access to a lawyer, especially for an individual
detained in a foreign country … .42
On the facts, the applicant had adduced “concrete and compelling evidence that his
co-defendants were tortured into providing the case against him.” He had also shown
that the relevant Jordanian State Security Court was “incapable of properly investigating
allegations of torture and excluding torture evidence … .” This material, in addition to
the extensive evidence in the domestic proceedings, sufficed to discharge the burden
of proof on the applicant to show the real risk of a flagrant denial of Art. 6 in Jordan, the
receiving state.43

The “flagrant denial of justice” in extraordinary rendition cases
The last set of cases concern states that have been complicit in extraordinary renditions.
The ECtHR defines “extraordinary rendition” as “extra-judicial transfer of persons from
one jurisdiction or State to another, for the purposes of detention and interrogation
outside the normal legal system, where there was a real risk of torture or cruel, inhuman
or degrading treatment.”44 Those practices may violate Art. 6 ECHR, along with Arts. 3, 5,
8, and 13, inter alia.
The key cases are Al Nashiri v. Poland, Husayn (Abu Zubaydah) v. Poland, and Al
Nashiri v. Romania.45 They concern the complicity of ECHR state parties in extraordinary
renditions conducted by the United States in its “war on terror.” In the two Polish cases,
the court found there was “little doubt” that the applicant detainees had been tortured
into providing self-incriminating statements, as had other witnesses for the prosecution.46
Furthermore, the court concluded that the US military commission, which was to try
the applicants, did not clearly provide “guarantees of impartiality and independence.”
It lacked “legitimacy” and a legal basis in United States and international law, and was
sufficiently likely to admit torture evidence.47
As the complicit ECHR state parties must have known that the suspected terrorists
would be tried in those proceedings, they violated Art. 6(1) ECHR.48 Indefinite detention
without charge or trial was also flagrantly unfair.49
Cooperative confiscation: “Flagrant denial” or “full review”?
The mentioned cases indicate that Art. 6 ECHR should require ECHR state parties to
consider the quality of the foreign proceedings in which confiscation is ordered; however,
the cases do not say when a requested state could breach Art. 6 ECHR by enforcing
an order that was unfair. What is the standard of care in reviewing the predicate foreign
proceedings?
In Soccoccia, the court refused to decide the level of review in the abstract because
the Austrian courts had “duly satisfied themselves, before authorising enforcement
of the forfeiture order, that the applicant had had a fair trial under United States law.”50
Furthermore, the court described its overall task as one of examining “whether the
Austrian courts, before authorising the enforcement of the forfeiture order, duly satisfied
themselves that the decision at issue was not the result of a flagrant denial of justice,”51
language reminiscent of the Soering case.
In Saccoccia, however, the ECtHR also cited Pellegrini v. Italy. There, the ECtHR had
found a violation of Art. 6(1) ECHR on the basis that the Italian courts had not “duly
satisfied themselves” that Vatican proceedings to annul a marriage were fair.52 That case
has been read to say that state parties must check for full compliance with Art. 6 ECHR
in a non-contracting country in civil enforcement proceedings.53 The ECtHR referenced
Pellegrini in Saccoccia, acknowledging that “courts may even be required to examine
whether the relevant proceedings fulfilled all the guarantees of Article 6.”54
In other cases, the court has presented Pelligrini as authority for a flexible, con-
text-specific standard,55 which requires full review of compliance with Art. 6 ECHR
when the execution of the foreign order would be of “paramount importance” to the
parties.56 The “flagrant denial” test, however, has also been applied in subsequent cases
on international cooperation in criminal and civil matters,57 as well as two judgments on
seizure and confiscation:
• Shorazova v. Malta: The ECtHR asked whether there could be a legitimate general
interest in seizing assets pursuant to a Kazakh request, given that any eventual
“confiscation would result from criminal proceedings which … may, or are likely to
consist of a flagrant denial of justice.”58 That statement was obiter. The court found
that the seizure process violated Art. 1 ECHR-P1 for other reasons.59

Requirements of the “flagrant denial test”
If the “flagrant denial” test does apply to confiscation cases, what does that test require of a
requested ECHR state party? Requests for the execution of foreign orders do not involve the
physical removal of a person from one state to another and concomitant risks of trial on the
basis of torture evidence. According to the court’s standard formulation, the complete denial
of the right to contest charges will amount to a flagrant denial of justice,62 as will “pro-
ceedings amounting to a mockery of basic fair trial principles.”63 The “flagrant denial” test
remains a “stringent” standard64 that will be breached only in “exceptional” circumstances.65
When does the flagrant denial test tend to be breached in confiscation matters?
While there are no cases directly on point, there are some potentially indicative
decisions. The first, Kislov v. Russia, was an unsuccessful challenge to an extradition
request that involved allegations of corruption in the requesting state.66 The ECtHR
refused to find that the applicant, who had himself previously raised concerns about
financial malfeasance, was the victim of a flagrant denial of justice in Belarus.67 The
court did not accept that the charges of bribery and forgery had been “fabricated” (that
is, that there was bad faith or insufficient evidence to extradite),68 nor could it find that
the Belarussian trial, although partially conducted in absentia, was flagrantly unfair.
The applicant’s departure from Belarus part-way through the trial was not evidently “on
account of any treatment in breach of Article 3 of the Convention, substantial grounds
relating to a real risk of such ill-treatment or any valid concern of political persecution
there … .”69 Thereafter, the applicant had had opportunities to participate in the proceed-
ings through his lawyer or from abroad.70 Other procedural irregularities, including his
trial by a judge who was subsequently sanctioned by the EU, were either not supported
by sufficient evidence or did not support a finding of conduct manifestly contrary to Art.
6 ECHR.71 Thus, “[w]hile … the proceedings in Belarus were not without reproach, it [was]
not … convincingly shown that they constituted a ‘flagrant denial of justice.’”72
Second, the court has refused to find a flagrant denial of justice in the use of irrebut-
table presumption in the foreign proceedings. The first applicant in Willcox and Hurford
v. United Kingdom was serving a sentence in the United Kingdom that had been imposed
by Thai courts.73 The Thai Narcotics Act of 1979 had deemed him in possession of drugs
for the purposes of distribution.74 There was no opportunity to rebut the presumption
under Thai law,75 hence, he claimed, his detention in the United Kingdom was arbitrary
because the underlying Thai sentence had been imposed in a procedure that was
flagrantly unfair.76 The ECtHR acknowledged “that there may be circumstances in which
a provision of the nature of section 15(3) of the Thai Act would be capable of giving rise
to an issue under [Art. 6(2) ECHR].”77 It found, though, that the applicant had received a
fair trial because the Thai prosecutor had discharged the burden of proving possession
within a lawful and otherwise fair procedure:

judges; he was present throughout the proceedings and was legally represented;
he was acquitted of some of the charges in accordance with the presumption of
innocence and, despite the fact that possession of heroin and ecstasy was not
contested, evidence was led to demonstrate that the drugs were in his possession;

Confiscation under EU Regulation 2018/1805
Given that the ECHR is a European treaty, the first issue is whether and how states that
are members of both the EU and Council of Europe are to assess requests for confis-
cation that relate to their obligations toward the EU. The EU’s Regulation 2018/1805
provides for the recognition of confiscation and freezing orders between member states,
as well as reasons for non-recognition and non-execution.83 The EU is not yet a party
to the ECHR, but all EU member states have ratified the ECHR, and the EU courts give a
special place to convention rights and obligations in interpreting EU general principles.84
Also, the EU has an extensive judicial apparatus in its court system for enforcing human
rights and freedoms.
That substantive and procedural apparatus is important in the ECtHR’s assessment
of interferences that flow from ECHR state parties’ “strict international legal obligations”
toward the EU.85 In those matters, the ECtHR applies a “rebuttable presumption of
equivalent protection.” State parties, that is, may presume the EU’s compliance with the
ECHR if (a) the state does not retain “any margin of manoeuvre” in how it implements its
EU obligations and (b) the state has “deploy[ed] … the full potential of the supervisory
mechanism provided for by European Union law” on the facts of the case.86 If those
conditions are met, the presumption can only be rebutted by evidence that protections
for human rights were “manifestly deficient” on the facts.87
The presumption of equivalent protection (or “Bosphorus” presumption) was applied
to the mutual recognition of a civil judgment in Avotiņš v. Latvia,88 which concerned the
enforcement of a Cypriot judgment in Latvia under the EU’s Brussels I Regulation of
2001.89 The Brussels I Regulation sought to provide an “efficient and rapid” procedure for
the enforcement of judgements issued within the EU.90 It contained grounds for non-en-
forcement that defendants could raise in challenges to enforcement decisions,91 but it
“allowed the refusal of recognition or enforcement of a foreign judgment only within very
precise limits and subject to certain preconditions.”92 Therefore, the requested state did
not retain discretion in its enforcement.
Avotiņš has been applied to the system of mutual recognition of arrest warrants
within the EU.93 It may also be illustrative of how the ECtHR will deal with complaints
about the enforcement of confiscation orders among EU states. There are differences
in the wording of Regulation 2018/1805 and the Brussels I Regulation, including their
exceptions. Nonetheless, it is possible after Avotiņš that the ECtHR may apply the
Bosphorus presumption to the mutual recognition regime for EU confiscation orders. If
so, EU member state parties to the ECHR “must give full effect to that mechanism where
the protection of convention rights cannot be considered manifestly deficient.”94

Similar considerations apply with respect to UNSC resolutions. Under Chapter vII of the
UN Charter, the UNSC has the power to “determine the existence of any threat to the
peace, breach of the peace, or act of aggression and shall make recommendations, or
decide what measures shall be taken … to maintain or restore international peace and
security.”95 Member states of the UN “agree to accept and carry out” the UNSC’s deci-

their other international obligations.97

Identifying punitive confiscation orders
The leading case is Welch v. United Kingdom.5 The applicant, Welch, had challenged
conviction-based confiscation orders that had been imposed on him under the Drug
Trafficking Offences Act of 1986.6 It was clear that the orders were retrospective and
that retrospective application was permitted by the act.7 The only question was whether
confiscation constituted a penalty within the meaning of Art. 7(1) ECHR. On this point,
the ECtHR held as follows:
The wording of Article 7 para. 1 … second sentence, indicates that the starting-
point in any assessment of the existence of a penalty is whether the measure in
question is imposed following conviction for a “criminal offence”. Other factors
that may be taken into account as relevant in this connection are the nature and
purpose of the measure in question; its characterisation under national law; the
procedures involved in the making and implementation of the measure; and its
severity.8
Applying those principles, the ECtHR observed that the confiscation order depended on
conviction, though it was calculated using a presumption of prior criminal conduct.9 Even
if it also had preventive and reparative goals, it had punitive objectives as indicated by10
• The use of “sweeping statutory assumptions” to calculate the applicant’s benefit from
crime;
• The application of the order to the proceeds, rather than the profits of crime;
• The relevance of the applicant’s culpability to the calculation of the amount of the
order; and
• The provision for imprisonment in default of payment.11
The reality for the applicant was a harsher sanction than was possible at the time of his
offenses.12 The confiscation order thus amounted to a penalty with retrospective applica-
tion that violated Art. 7(1).
The relative importance of conviction
Subsequent cases have confirmed that conviction is a relevant but not a decisive factor
in determining whether confiscation orders are punitive and thus within the scope of Art.
7(1) ECHR.

THE RIGHT TO PUNISHMENT UNDER LAW
Non-punitive but conviction-based confiscation orders
In Dassa Foundation and Others v. Liechtenstein and Ulemek v. Serbia, the ECtHR
found conviction-based confiscation orders to have a preventive or remedial nature
or purpose. In these cases, it was important that the orders were not calculated using
assumptions or considerations of the offender’s culpability.13 The orders targeted the
“actual enrichment of the beneficiary of the offence” rather than the net proceeds
of crime14 and thus the orders were not enforceable by a prison term in default of
payment.15 The national courts unanimously agreed about the non-punitive character
of a confiscation orders,16 the ECtHR describing the orders (in Ulemek) as more akin
to “civil forfeiture in rem rather than a fine … .”17 It was also relevant that the funds
obtained by the state in payment of the order could be used to benefit victims,18 that
the laws underlying the order implemented international treaties against money
laundering,19 and that specific orders were based on evidence of illicit acquisition
that had not been rebutted.20 As in other cases,21 confiscation was not an automatic
consequences of conviction but followed from further processes of assessment by
domestic authorities outside the standard criminal process.22
The severity (value) of an order has not been decisive,23 at least insofar as the order
applies to assets that constituted (or represented) the actual enrichment from crime24 or
whose “legal origins” could not be ascertained.25
Identifying punitive but NCB confiscation orders
In NCB confiscation cases, the absence of a conviction is likewise a relevant but not
decisive factor in the characterization of an order as a penalty under the second sen-
tence of Art. 7(1) ECHR. The ECtHR considers all the Welch criteria, giving weight to the
classifications of the confiscation order in domestic law, along with other indicators of
its nature and purpose(s).
The leading case is G.I.E.M. S.r.l.,26 which also elaborated the requirements of Art.
7(1) ECHR when a penalty is established. The final judgment in a series of cases
before the ECtHR, G.I.E.M. S.r.l. was a challenge to NCB confiscation orders that had
been imposed on an individual and related companies in connection with the crime
of unlawful site development.27 None of the applicants had been convicted of those
offenses,28 but applying Welch, the Grand Chamber confirmed that conviction was not
the sole indicator of a penalty and that a NCB confiscation order could amount to a
penalty on other facts.29
In this case, the provision governing confiscation had been given the heading
“Criminal Sanctions” in the construction code.30 Both the Italian Court of Cassation and
the Italian government had emphasized the punitive and deterrent nature and purpose
of the orders during the litigation.31 Confiscation was also mandatory and independent
of any risk to the environment or activity on the land.32 The confiscation was “particular-
ly harsh and intrusive” because it enabled non-compensable taking of all plots of land
within a site and not just the land that had been or was to be developed.33 Finally, the
court observed that the criminal courts were responsible for ordering the confiscation
in general and in that case.34

The requirements of Art. 7(1) ECHR
If a domestic confiscation order is a penalty, it must be foreseeable. The ECtHR is both
permissive and strict in how it interprets this rule. On the one hand, the court looks at the
substance of the law that was operative at the time of an offense. Even if a confiscation
provision was retrospective, a confiscation order may have been foreseeable under other
rules that existed at that time.47 On the other hand, Art. 7(1) ECHR goes beyond a ban

makes clear, penalties may only be imposed on persons who have been found liable for
criminal offenses or third parties who were allowed to participate in those proceedings.48
Cooperative confiscation orders under Art. 7(1) ECHR
How does Art. 7(1) ECHR apply to orders to enforce foreign confiscation orders that are
penalties? In Saccoccia, the applicant challenged the exequatur order on the basis that
money laundering had not been criminal in Austria at the time of his offenses.49 The
ECtHR dismissed this complaint on the basis that the Austrian enforcement order was
not a penalty but a means of executing a foreign penalty.50 Moreover, forfeiture was a
foreseeable penalty for money laundering under US law at the time of his offense.51 The
decision in Saccoccia thus indicates that orders to enforce foreign penalties are outside
the scope of Art. 7(1) ECHR, at least when the penalty could be foreseen in the request-

4 The Right to Property in
Art. 1 ECHR-P1
Overview
In the Council of Europe’s human rights system, the right to property is guaranteed
by Art. 1 ECHR-P1. The two paragraphs of that article apply when state parties to the
protocol deprive natural or legal persons of their “property” (“possessions”) or otherwise
control or interfere with those rights. Such interferences must be lawful and proportion-
ate to a “public” or “general interest.”
This chapter finds that the ECtHR has adopted a broad concept of property under
Art. 1 ECHR-P1, with the consequence that it has considered many confiscation orders
to target “possessions” under Art. 1 ECHR-P1. The key question in asset recovery cases
is rather whether confiscation orders are lawful and justified interferences with property
rights. The chapter finds that states have a wide margin of appreciation to protect the
community through confiscation and related acts of cooperation under Art. 1 ECHR-P1.
Nonetheless, state parties to the protocol must ensure that their confiscation orders are
lawful and that they do not impose an “individual and excessive burden” on an applicant,
given their procedure, scope, and context. The court’s case law on Art. 6 and Art. 7
ECHR is relevant but not determinative of these issues.
In short, the right to property is available in many (if not all) confiscation cases.
Its dual requirement of lawfulness and proportionality qualify the power of states to
confiscate illicit wealth without a clear legal basis and require attention to the “fair
balance” between collective and individual interests. Article 14 and Art. 18 ECHR are
comparatively niche issues but warrant consideration given the targeted nature of some
asset recovery laws and the political circumstances of many asset recovery cases.
Key points
• The ECtHR has held a broad range of confiscation orders to interfere with “posses-
sions,” under Art. 1 ECHR-P1.
• Such confiscation orders are generally dealt with as a control of the use of property
under the third rule of Art. 1 ECHR-P1. Exceptionally, the ECtHR has dispensed with
the need for classification or applied a different rule to confiscation orders.
• All confiscation orders that are within the scope of Art. 1 ECHR must be lawful—that
is, they must have a basis in a law of the confiscating state that itself adheres to the
principles of the rule of law: accessibility, precision, and foreseeability.
• A lawful confiscation order must also be proportionate to a public (or general) interest
that it seeks to secure. Within the margin of appreciation, the court will consider
the safeguards in the confiscation procedure, the local and international context for
confiscation, and the size and scope of the confiscation order, inter alia.

The concept of an “interference”
The second issue is whether and how asset recovery proceedings interfere with prop-

distinct rules.” These rules correspond to three forms of interference with possessions:
The first rule, which is of a general nature, enounces the principle of peaceful
enjoyment of property; it is set out in the first sentence of the first paragraph. The
second rule covers deprivation of possessions and subjects it to certain conditions;
it appears in the second sentence of the same paragraph. The third rule recognises
that the [contracting states] are entitled, amongst other things, to control the use
of property in accordance with the general interest, by enforcing such laws as they
deem necessary for the purpose; it is contained in the second paragraph.23
The court typically deals with confiscation orders under the third rule of Art. 1 ECHR-P1.24

cash in administrative offense proceedings for non-declaration offenses.27

The lawfulness requirement
What does “lawfulness” entail? The third issue is whether the confiscation order is lawful
under Art. 1 ECHR-P1. Lawfulness is “first and most important” a requirement of the
right to property.40 Article 1 ECHR-P1, paragraph 1, requires deprivations of possessions
to be “subject to conditions provided for by law and by the general principles of interna-
tional law.” Paragraph 2 of Art. 1 ECHR-P1 allows for the enforcement of “laws” to control
of the use of possession, and so on.
The concept of lawfulness
The lawfulness requirement of Art. 1 ECHR-P1 has two main components.41
First, an interference must have a basis in the law of the interfering state party.42
The term “law” includes “binding legislative acts, judge-made rules, and subordinate
executive regulations,”43 as well as international agreements that have effect within the
domestic legal order.44 The meaning of domestic laws is to be determined by domestic
courts. The ECtHR is not a “fourth chamber.” It will accept interpretations of domestic
courts45 unless those decisions “appear arbitrary or manifestly unreasonable … or …
have been issued in ‘flagrant denial of justice.’”46
Second, the underlying law must itself adhere to the principles of the rule of law:
accessibility, precision, and foreseeability.47 These requirements are interlinked, and their
focus is substantive rather than formal.48 Laws must be available to the public, even if
they are not published in an official government gazette;49 laws must also be written in
way that allows citizens to reasonably predict the consequences of their actions, even
with legal advice50 and elaboration by the courts.51 Procedural considerations also come
into play since a lack of safeguards may render a process arbitrary.52
The lawfulness requirement in domestic confiscation cases
The ECtHR generally accepts that respondent states act lawfully in domestic confis-
cation cases. Exceptionally, the court has upheld challenges to confiscation orders
because their legal basis had ceased to exist53 or was not cited by the respondent coun-
try.54 In cases involving the restraint of property, interferences have become unlawful
when domestic authorities have failed to comply with their own court rulings to return
goods.55 Box 4.1 provides a more general illustration of the lawfulness requirement.

Box 4.5. Discriminatory and politically motivated confiscation orders
The state’s reasons for confiscating property are also relevant to the application of Art. 14 and Art. 18
ECHR, which prohibit discrimination and abuses of process in the restriction of convention rights and
freedoms.
Discriminatory confiscation orders under Art. 14 ECHR
Article 14 ECHR prohibits discriminatory interferences with the substantive guarantees in the convention
and its protocols.a It requires that state parties “secur[e] [those rights and freedoms] without discrimi-
nation on any ground such as sex, race, colour, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or
social origin, association with a national minority, property, birth or other status.” It follows that state
parties must ensure that interferences with protected interests are non-discriminatory or that any
discrimination is justified.
In asset recovery cases, Art. 14 has special relevance to the confiscation and seizure laws that apply
specifically to public officials, their family members, and associates. “Other status” in Art. 14 captures
“an identifiable, objective or personal characteristic,”b like a person’s occupation of high office,c former
employment in the civil serviced or in a secret service organization,e or family relationships.f Article 14
ECHR will also apply to differential treatment under general purpose cooperation or confiscation laws
that attach to a person’s “other status” or one of the “badges”g of discrimination in Art. 14 (such as race,
political opinion, minority status, and so on).
If the confiscation is discriminatory, the discrimination must be a proportionate means to pursue a
legitimate aim.h States have a margin of appreciation under Art. 14 ECHR, which is especially wide “when
it comes to general measures of economic or social strategy”i or interferences under the Art. 1 ECHR-P1,
second paragraph (controls of the use of possessions).j “The ECtHR has found the responsibilities of
high office and the gravity of an offense to be acceptable justifications for discrimination in the applica-
tion of substantive guarantees.”k
Political prosecutions and confiscation orders under Art. 18 ECHR
Whereas Art. 14 ECHR bans discrimination in the protection of human rights, Art. 18 ECHR prohibits
ECHR state parties from restricting convention rights and freedoms “for any purpose other than those
for which they have been prescribed.” It seeks to stop ECHR state parties from abusing their power to
restrict convention rights and freedoms.
While Art. 18 has been raised successfully by former politically exposed persons, public officials,
and political figures,l it will not give rise to a finding of breach unless the state’s “predominant purpose”
in restricting a right was incompatible with a substantive guarantee of the convention.m There are no
special rules for proving state motivations: an applicant may rely on various forms of evidence and the
ECtHR uses its usual standard of proof (“beyond reasonable doubt”) to assess those facts.n
That standard of proof “is not co-extensive with that of the national legal systems which employ it.”o It
was met in Democracy and Human Rights Resource Centre and Mustafeyev v. Azerbaijan, a case regarding
the freezing of bank accounts of human rights activists.p The court also accepts, though, that prosecu-
tions of former leaders may legitimately seek to call such people to justice,q while general indications of
political goals will not meet the predominant motivation test.r
There is also a difference between the tests for state abuses of process under Art. 18 ECHR and the
“politically motivated prosecution” exception to international cooperation in criminal matters. In the key
case of Merabishvili v. Georgia, the ECtHR clarified that “the extradition courts were in essence assessing
a future risk, whereas the [ECtHR under Art. 18 ECHR] is concerned with past facts.”s Partly for that
reason, the ECtHR did not follow UK, French, and Greek judges who had prohibited extradition of other
senior figures in the applicant’s party back to Georgia.t

Size and scope
The size and scope of the confiscation order are also relevant to the assessment of
proportionality. They are assessed with regard to nature and purpose of the confiscation
order and the offense.
As to the nature of the order, the ECtHR has accepted that the courts may confiscate
the available proceeds and not merely the profits of crime when confiscation is a
punishment for a serious predicate offense like drug trafficking.101 Similarly, the court
has found that in rem confiscation orders, which remove the actual or transformed
proceeds of crime, may be proportionate to those orders’ restorative or compensatory
goals.102 Crimes covered by a proceeds of crime statute should be acquisitive in nature
and sufficiently serious to justify such quasi-criminal regulation, rather than tax or social
security laws.103
As to the nature of the offense, in cases concerning the confiscation of proceeds, the
ECtHR considers whether the triggering offenses were liable to generate profits104 and
the size of those profits in the applicant’s case.105 When considering the confiscation
of instrumentalities and objects of offenses, the court has mentioned the gravity of
the predicate (triggering) offense to confiscation106 and the commensurability of that
crime to the value of the assets.107 In cases involving the confiscation of undisclosed but
lawfully acquired cash, it has considered the (small) damage to the interest of the state
compared to considerable loss suffered by applicants,108 as well as the degree of candor
on the part of the applicant.109
Retrospectivity
As for retrospectivity, it is not per se disproportionate for state parties to impose con-
fiscation laws on past events. However, the broad temporal scope of such orders may
contribute to a finding of arbitrariness together with other factors. Thus, in Todorov, the
court found that the wide temporal scope of the confiscation laws, which had a 25-year
statute of limitation, together with the low quantitative threshold for the operation of a
presumption of illicit acquisition, and the presence of other rules that assisted the state
in establishing the applicants’ lack of entitlement to property “in t heir cumulative effect
could be such as to tilt the balance in the proceedings in the State’s favour.”110
Local and international context
Finally, the circumstances that led to the introduction of a confiscation law may also be
relevant to its proportionality. International support for anticorruption reforms and major
political transitions in a respondent state may weigh in favor of proportionality if confis-
cation orders correspond to those recommendations and are otherwise fair.111 That said,
the mere fact that multilateral conventions encourage swift and extensive cooperation
does not remove the obligation on their parties to ensure that acts of assistance comply
with “international human rights standards.”112

ECHR, Art. 6—Right to a fair trial
1. In the determination of his civil rights and obligations or of any criminal charge
against him, everyone is entitled to a fair and public hearing within a reasonable
time by an independent and impartial tribunal established by law. Judgment shall
be pronounced publicly but the press and public may be excluded from all or part of
the trial in the interests of morals, public order or national security in a democratic
society, where the interests of juveniles or the protection of the private life of the
parties so require, or to the extent strictly necessary in the opinion of the court in
special circumstances where publicity would prejudice the interests of justice.
2. Everyone charged with a criminal offence shall be presumed innocent until proved
guilty according to law.
3. Everyone charged with a criminal offence has the following minimum rights:
a. to be informed promptly, in a language which he understands and in detail, of the
nature and cause of the accusation against him;
b. to have adequate time and facilities for the preparation of his defence;
c. to defend himself in person or through legal assistance of his own choosing or, if
he has not sufficient means to pay for legal assistance, to be given it free when the
interests of justice so require;
d. to examine or have examined witnesses against him and to obtain the attendance
and examination of witnesses on his behalf under the same conditions as witness-
es against him;
e. to have the free assistance of an interpreter if he cannot understand or speak the
language used in court.
ECHR, Art. 7—No punishment without law
1. No one shall be held guilty of any criminal offence on account of any act or omission

ECHR, Art. 14—Prohibition of discrimination
The enjoyment of the rights and freedoms set forth in this Convention shall be secured
without discrimination on any ground such as sex, race, colour, language, religion,
political or other opinion, national or social origin, association with a national minority,
property, birth or other status.
ECHR, Art. 18—Limitation on use of restrictions on
rights
The restrictions permitted under this Convention to the said rights and freedoms shall
not be applied for any purpose other than those for which they have been prescribed.
ECHR-P1, Art. 1—Protection of property
1. Every natural or legal person is entitled to the peaceful enjoyment of his possessions.
No one shall be deprived of his possessions except in the public interest and subject
to the conditions provided for by law and by the general principles of international law.
2. The preceding provisions shall not, however, in any way impair the right of a State to
enforce such laws as it deems necessary to control the use of property in accordance
with the general interest or to secure the payment of taxes or other contributions or
penalties.
ECHR-P7, Art. 4—Right not to be tried or punished
twice
1. No one shall be liable to be tried or punished again in criminal proceedings under the
jurisdiction of the same State for an offence for which he has already been finally
acquitted or convicted in accordance with the law and penal procedure of that State.
2. The provisions of the preceding paragraph shall not prevent the reopening of the case
in accordance with the law and penal procedure of the State concerned, if there is
evidence of new or newly discovered facts, or if there has been a fundamental defect
in the previous proceedings, which could affect the outcome of the case.
3. No derogation from this Article shall be made under Article 15 of the Convention.
