1 Background 
1.1 Introduction 
The Transparency, Integrity and Accountability Programme (TIP) supports state and non-state 
actors with the implementation of the National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS) in South 
Africa. The TIP is implemented by the Deutsche Gesellschaft fr Internationale 
Zusammenarbeit (GIZ) GmbH on behalf of the German Federal Ministry for Economic 
Cooperation and Development (BMZ).1 Building sustainable institutional capacity is one of the 
areas of support for the implementation of the NACS. In this regard, TIPS is providing capacity 
development and technical support to the Presidency and the Department of Planning, 
Monitoring and Evaluation (DPME), and the National Anti-Corruption Council (NACAC).  
DPME is the lead Executing Agency for TIP and requested assistance to support its monitoring 
and coordination function, through financing the position of an anti-corruption monitoring 
and evaluation expert. GIZ appointed Centre for Learning on Evaluation and Results in 
Anglophone Africa (CLEAR-AA) of the University of the Witwatersrand to support the DPME 
Senior Expert: Anti-corruption M&E.  
This Landscape Analysis report represents the second key deliverable of CLEAR-AA, as set out 
in the Inception Report (5 June 2024). It supplements the work of Mthente Research and 
Consulting Services who had previously been contracted to contribute to the development of 
the M&E system for the NACS.2 
There was broad consensus that the initial Landscape Analysis report of Mthente was 
comprehensive and effectively captured the key elements of the anti-corruption landscape in 
South Africa. However, in order to refine the Monitoring, Evaluation, and Learning (MEL) 
system and ensure alignment with recent developments, additional sections have been 
included. These include updates on stakeholder dynamics, legislative amendments, and 
international cooperation frameworks. Incorporating these elements strengthens the 
analytical base for the National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS) Monitoring and Evaluation 
system by addressing new priorities and highlighting emerging trends critical for strategic 
implementation. 
1.2 Purpose, scope and objectives 
The purpose of this Landscape Analysis is to provide a nuanced understanding of the current 
anti-corruption ecosystem in South Africa, building on the foundational work conducted by 
Mthente Research and Consulting Services. This report aims to complement the previous 
literature review by filling gaps and integrating recent developments. The key focus is not only 
on outlining the broader ecosystem but also on understanding the Monitoring and Evaluation 
(M&E) roles of various stakeholders. By identifying who is actively engaged in M&E activities, 
this report seeks to prevent duplication of efforts and ensure that new interventions add value 
to the National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS). Ultimately, these insights are essential for 
designing an effective M&E system that aligns with the strategic priorities of the NACS and 
strengthens the overall anti-corruption framework.

The objectives of the Landscape Analysis are to: 
1. Supplement the existing literature review by incorporating updated information 
where relevant. 
2. Provide an overview of the current country context in terms of policy, legislation, 
and political shifts, particularly reflecting on the political transition to a Government

3. Map key stakeholders and their roles/interests in the implementation, monitoring,

NACS Indicator Framework and future monitoring efforts. 
5. Highlight evolving stakeholder dynamics, legislative updates, and international 
cooperation that are influencing anti-corruption strategies, including the 2024 
amendments to the Prevention and Combating of Corruption Activities Act and 
enhanced whistleblower protections. 
These objectives are not just about understanding the ecosystem, but are crucial for designing 
an effective M&E system for the NACS. By identifying current M&E practices and stakeholders, 
we can ensure that the new system complements existing efforts, fills gaps, and adds unique 
value to the anti-corruption landscape in South Africa. 
By incorporating these elements, this report aims to provide a refreshed and comprehensive 
understanding of the anti-corruption landscape in South Africa, building on and diverging from 
previous reviews where new insights have emerged.

2 Political context 
2.1 Political transition and its Impact on anti-corruption strategies 
Corruption remains a significant challenge for South Africa, as evidenced by international 
measures such as the Transparency International Corruption Perception Index and the 
World Banks Governance Indicators, which consistently highlight the pervasiveness of 
corruption in the country. The findings of the Zondo Commission of Inquiry into State Capture 
further underscored the entrenched nature of corruption within state institutions, many of 
which were weakened or hollowed out during this period (Transparency International, 2023 
and World Bank, 2023). 
The transition to a Government of National Unity (GNU) in South Africa following the May 
2024 elections introduced both opportunities and challenges for advancing anti-corruption 
efforts. With a coalition of ten political parties now sharing executive authority, the GNU 
provides a unique platform to demonstrate political will in prioritizing anti-corruption. The 
collective leadership of the GNU, especially its first Cabinet Lekgotla in July 2024, has the 
potential to align various political factions behind a unified anti-corruption agenda (The 
Presidency, 2024). This creates an opportunity for broad-based political support for the 
National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS) and the enhancement of whistleblower protections, 
both of which are recognized priorities within the coalition. 
However, the GNU also presents challenges, particularly in maintaining sustained and 
cohesive political will. The fragmentation of political authority among different parties with 
divergent policy priorities risks undermining the long-term implementation of anti-corruption 
measures. The possibility of competing agendas within the coalition could dilute the focus on 
critical anti-corruption interventions such as the establishment of a single, independent anti-
corruption agency. Furthermore, the transition of leadership within key ministries might 
disrupt the continuity needed for the effective execution of the NACS, especially where anti-
corruption efforts intersect with other governance reforms. 
Despite these challenges, the GNUs collective leadership offers significant opportunities for 
collaboration and building cross-party consensus on anti-corruption. For instance, political 
manifestos across various coalition parties emphasized the need for stronger enforcement 
mechanisms and independent anti-corruption agencies. This political alignment, if managed 
strategically, could foster a stronger commitment to implementing key elements of the NACS, 
such as reforming the public procurement system and improving whistleblower protections. 
To capitalize on the opportunities presented by the GNU, it is crucial to ensure that anti-
corruption efforts are institutionalized within long-term frameworks such as the Medium-
Term Development Plan (MTDP) and the revised Annual Performance Plans (APPs) of 
government departments. Furthermore, transparency in monitoring the implementation of 
these plans will be essential to maintaining public trust and ensuring accountability, 
particularly as the GNU seeks to address the deep-rooted challenges of corruption uncovered 
by the Zondo Commission. 
While Mthentes review focused on the political context leading up to 2022, this analysis 
reflects the current priorities and challenges facing the GNU, particularly in relation to anti-
corruption strategies. One of the key areas of debate within the GNU is the establishment of 
a single anti-corruption agency, with differing views on its structure and mandate. 
Furthermore, there is a renewed emphasis on enhanced whistleblower protections,

By supplementing previous analyses, this report provides updated insights into how this new

The Medium-Term Development Plan presents an opportunity to strengthen the fight 
against corruption. From analysis of political party manifestos, there appears to be broad 
consensus that corruption must be addressed. There are however different views on how this 
should be done. The following are the key messages contained in the manifestos of the 
political parties in the GNU: 
i. Strong anti-corruption agencies: Manifestos prioritised strengthening enforcement, 
investigation and prosecution of corruption. There were proposals for establishing a 
single anti-corruption agency to overcome the problem of poor coordination among 
existing agencies. There were calls for ensuring the independence of anti-corruption 
agencies and better funding for these agencies to carry out their mandates. 
ii. Whistle-blower protection: The need to strengthen protection for whistle-blowers 
was identified in manifestos. The existing protections were deemed to be insufficient 
as demonstrated by whistle-blowers who had been killed or suffered other forms of 
reprisal. 
iii. Ethical governance and professional conduct: Manifestos identified the need to 
ensure that politicians behaved ethically, and that Accounting Officers should be 
protected from political interference. Public servants found wanting should be 
subjected to consequence management. 
iv. Reform the public procurement system:  The Zondo Commission identified the public 
procurement system as a key enabler of theft of state resources. Manifestos identified 
the need to address the systemic weaknesses in the public procurement system, have 
greater transparency in public procurement, and introduce electronic procurement 
to reduce human interference in the procurement process. 
None of what is stated in the election manifestos is new. Many of the proposals already exist 
in the National Anti-Corruption Strategy. The challenge ahead lies in implementation.  
 
2.2 Implications of the Government of National Unity (GNU) for NACS 
implementation 
The political landscape of South Africa has undergone significant changes since the 
completion of Mthentes review, particularly with the transition to a Government of National 
Unity (GNU) following the May 2024 elections. The establishment of the GNU presents both 
opportunities and challenges for the implementation of the National Anti-Corruption Strategy 
(NACS). On one hand, the inclusion of multiple political parties in the executive branch may 
foster broader political support for anti-corruption measures. On the other hand, several 
challenges must be considered in light of this new political environment. 
1. Fragmented political will: The GNU comprises a coalition of ten political parties, each 
with its own priorities and approaches to governance. This could result in

fragmentation of political support for the NACS, especially if anti-corruption measures 
conflict with other policy agendas or interests within the coalition. 
2. Policy discontinuity: Changes in political leadership and party control over key 
ministerial portfolios could disrupt the continuity of the NACS. The success of the 
NACS relies on long-term, sustained commitment, and any shifts in political focus 
could delay or undermine its implementation. 
3. Opportunities for collaboration: Despite these challenges, the GNU provides an 
opportunity for enhanced collaboration between political actors. Corruption is 
identified as a priority in many political party manifestos, creating potential for a 
unified approach to combat corruption. If all parties within the GNU commit to 
implementing the NACS, it could benefit from increased political legitimacy. 
To mitigate these risks, it is critical that the NACS Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) system is 
designed to adapt to potential changes in political will. Mechanisms should be established to 
ensure that progress is tracked consistently and transparently, even if leadership or party 
priorities shift. 
2.3 Fluctuating political will as a risk factor 
One of the key risks identified in the Inception Report is the potential fluctuation in political 
will to support the implementation of the NACS. The establishment of the GNU introduces 
both opportunities and challenges in this regard. While the inclusion of multiple political 
parties may create broader support for anti-corruption efforts, it also increases the risk of 
fragmentation or shifts in focus as political leadership changes. 
To mitigate this risk, several strategies are recommended: 
 Engagement with Multiple Stakeholders: The NACS implementation team should 
engage a wide range of political actors within the GNU to ensure sustained support 
for the strategy. This includes working closely with parliamentary committees, 
provincial leaders, and non-governmental organizations to build consensus around 
the importance of the NACS. 
 Independent Oversight Mechanisms: Establishing independent bodies, such as civil 
society-led monitoring groups, to track the implementation of the NACS could help 
maintain momentum and accountability, even in the face of fluctuating political will. 
 Transparent Reporting: The M&E system should include transparent reporting 
mechanisms that allow the public to track progress on the NACS, creating external 
pressure on political leaders to maintain their commitment to anti-corruption 
initiatives. 
2.4 Ensuring the long-term sustainability of the NACS 
The long-term sustainability of the NACS under the GNU presents a complex challenge. 
Coalition governments are often characterized by shifting political priorities and 
compromises, which could impact the sustained implementation of long-term initiatives like 
the NACS. To address this, the following measures should be considered: 
1. Institutionalizing Anti-Corruption Measures: Embedding anti-corruption measures 
within broader institutional frameworks can help insulate them from changes in 
political leadership. Integrating the NACS Indicator Framework into national

2. Strengthening Cross-Party Collaboration: Building alliances across political parties to

3. Enhancing Public Accountability: Encouraging public oversight and accountability can

2.5 Risk Assessment: Fluctuating political will 
The risk of fluctuating political will remains one of the greatest challenges for the 
implementation of the NACS under the GNU. Political leaders may face competing priorities 
that reduce their commitment to anti-corruption efforts, or they may come under pressure 
from stakeholders who benefit from maintaining the status quo. The presence of multiple 
political parties with differing views on anti-corruption efforts exacerbates this risk. 
To mitigate these risks, the following strategies should be considered: 
 Bipartisan Support for NACS: Ensuring that the NACS and its M&E system have 
support from across the political spectrum will protect the strategy from leadership 
changes or shifts in party priorities. This can be achieved through public consultations, 
parliamentary discussions, and stakeholder engagement that includes 
representatives from all major political parties. 
 Engaging Key Political Leaders: Publicly engaging political leaders in discussions about 
the importance of the NACS and its role in strengthening governance and restoring 
public trust can build sustained political will. Leaders who make public commitments 
to anti-corruption efforts will be more likely to maintain their support for the M&E 
system. 
 Monitoring Political Commitment: The M&E system should include indicators that 
track political commitment to anti-corruption efforts, providing civil society and the 
media with tools to hold political leaders accountable for their commitments.

3 Regulatory framework for anti-corruption 
South Africa has enacted several pieces of legislation and introduced policies to prevent and 
combat corruption in the public and private sector. This section of the landscape analysis 
provides a brief overview of the regulatory framework for anti-corruption. 
3.1 National Development Plan and Revised Medium-Term Strategic 
Framework 
National Development Plan 2030  
The National Development Plan released in 2012, identified corruption as a risk to good 
governance, which undermines national development. The diagnostic work undertaken by the 
National Planning Commission found that South Africa suffered from high levels of corruption, 
undermining the rule of law and hindering social and economic transformation. The 
Commission identified four areas in which policy interventions were required: 
i. Build a resilient anti-corruption system 
ii. Strengthen accountability and responsibility of public servants 
iii. Create a transparent, responsive and accountable public service 
iv. Strengthen judicial governance and the rule of law 
In building a resilient anti-corruption system, the National Development Plan proposed the 
following: 
i. Strengthen the multi-agency anti-corruption system  
ii. Strengthen the protection of whistle-blowers  
iii. Greater central oversight over the awarding of large tenders or tenders with long 
duration  
iv. Empower the tender compliance monitoring office to investigate corruption and the 
value for money of tenders 
The National Planning Commission emphasised that corruption was a societal problem that 
required commitment from all members of society to prevent and combat corruption. 
Revised Medium-Term Strategic Framework (2019-2024) 
The Revised Medium-Term Strategic Framework, (MTSF) which represents the Governments 
five-year development plan, identified Priority 1: A capable, ethical and developmental state 
as one of its seven apex priorities. This priority is also seen as the underpinning of the other 
MSTF priorities. In its elaboration of an ethical state, the MTSF describes it as one driven 
by constitutional principles and values of public administration and the rule of law, focused 
on the progressive realization of the socio-economic rights and social justice outlined in the 
Bill of Rights.3

South Africa has introduced several legislative changes aimed at reinforcing its anti-corruption 
framework. These developments align with the goals of the National Anti-Corruption Strategy 
(NACS) and strengthen both public and private sector accountability. The key legislative 
updates include the Public Service Amendment Bill, the Electoral Matters Amendment Act, 
the Companies Amendment Bill, and the Public Procurement Bill. 
1. Public Service Amendment Bill 
 Key Provisions: The Public Service Amendment Bill (passed in 2024) seeks to amend 
the 1994 Public Service Act. It provides for the devolution of administrative powers 
from executive authorities to heads of departments, thereby reducing bottlenecks 
and improving accountability at the department level. 
 Impact on Anti-Corruption: By delegating more authority to departmental heads, the 
bill aims to streamline decision-making and enhance managerial responsibility. This is 
expected to reduce opportunities for corruption by ensuring closer oversight at the 
operational level.

 Current Status: The bill was passed by Parliament in the first half of 2024 and is now 
in the implementation phase. 
 Significance for NACS: This bill supports NACSs Pillar on Professional Conduct, 
which emphasizes improving public service integrity. However, challenges may arise 
in ensuring uniform implementation across departments, especially in smaller or 
under-resourced units. 
2. Electoral Matters Amendment Act 
 Main Changes: The Electoral Matters Amendment Act introduces significant reforms 
aimed at enhancing transparency in political party funding. It criminalizes donations 
made to gain political influence, making it a punishable offense with fines or 
imprisonment (Republic of South Africa, 2024). 
 Addressing Political Party Funding: This act is a direct response to the influence of 
money in politics, where donations were historically used to gain undue political 
favour. The reforms are crucial for mitigating corruption risks associated with political 
patronage. 
 Timeline: The act was assented in June 2024, just after the national elections. It is

4.4 National Anti-Corruption Strategy 2020-2030 
4.4.1 An overview of NACS 
The National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS) 2020-2030 represents South Africa's blueprint 
to combat corruption within its borders. The NACS comprises six strategic pillars, each with 
anticipated impacts or objectives, outcomes and implementation programmes. The 
development of NACS followed an extensive consultative process, to build broad ownership 
and alignment with existing anti-corruption measures. The strategy places a strong emphasis 
on prevention and early detection of corrupt practices through interventions that promote 
good governance, transparency, integrity and accountability. These measures are 
complementary to the reactive measures of law enforcement agencies. The strategy calls for 
a whole-of-society approach that engages government, state institutions supporting 
democracy, civil society organisations, the private sector, and other non-state actors. The 
NACS promotes active citizenry empowered to hold leaders and organizations accountable, 
and foresees a state where transparency is not just a principle but a practised norm.  
The expected result of the successful implementation of the NACS is significantly reduced 
levels of corruption, and improved investor and public confidence in South Africa. Some of 
the objective measures of success include better audit outcomes for state institutions, 
improved ratings in the corruption perception index, reduction in illicit financial flows, and 
citizen trust in institutions.12

4.4.2 Developing a Theory of Change for NACS 
The Landscape Analysis has identified a critical gap in the National Anti-Corruption Strategy 
(NACS)—the absence of an explicit Theory of Change (ToC). A ToC is essential for mapping out 
the pathways through which NACS interventions are expected to lead to desired anti-
corruption outcomes. To fill this gap, the following elements should be considered in the 
development of a comprehensive ToC for the NACS: 
 Causal Pathways: The ToC should clearly map out the relationships between NACS 
interventions and their intended outcomes. For example, legislative reforms aimed at 
strengthening whistleblower protections should be linked to increased reporting of 
corruption, which in turn leads to more effective enforcement and reduced instances 
of corrupt practices. Similarly, efforts to promote transparency in public procurement 
should lead to improved public trust in institutions and a reduction in fraudulent 
contracts. 
 Mechanisms of Change: Identifying the mechanisms of change is critical to 
understanding how NACS interventions will produce the desired results. Mechanisms 
could include capacity-building initiatives that strengthen the ability of law 
enforcement agencies to investigate and prosecute corruption cases, or public 
education campaigns that raise awareness of citizens rights to report corrupt 
practices.

4.4.3 Potential pathways and mechanisms of change 
As part of the Theory of Change for the NACS, the following pathways and mechanisms of 
change are proposed to guide the achievement of the strategys desired outcomes: 
 Pathway for Prevention: One of the key pathways to reducing corruption involves 
raising public awareness about the dangers and costs of corruption. Public education 
campaigns, coupled with stronger legal protections for whistleblowers, can lead to 
increased citizen engagement in reporting corruption. As more citizens come forward 
with reports, public institutions will face greater pressure to adopt transparent 
practices, leading to a reduction in opportunities for corrupt activities. 
 Pathway for Enforcement: Strengthening the enforcement of anti-corruption laws 
requires building the capacity of institutions such as the Special Investigating Unit 
(SIU) and the National Prosecuting Authority (NPA). By improving coordination 
between law enforcement agencies, enhancing the skills of investigators and 
prosecutors, and ensuring that cases are handled efficiently, the deterrent effect of 
enforcement actions will be amplified. This should lead to a measurable reduction in 
corruption-related crimes. 
 Mechanism for Public Accountability: Transparency is a key mechanism for holding 
public officials accountable. The implementation of open data platforms, where 
information on government spending, public procurement, and financial disclosures 
is made publicly available, can empower civil society organizations and the media to 
monitor government actions. This enhanced scrutiny will serve as a deterrent to 
corrupt behaviour and promote a culture of accountability across public institutions. 
By incorporating these pathways and mechanisms of change into the NACS Theory of Change, 
the strategy will be better positioned to achieve its long-term objectives of reducing 
corruption and improving public trust in governance.

assumptions that underpin the success of the strategy. The following key assumptions must

 Assumption of Political will: The success of the NACS depends on sustained political 
will from government leaders and policymakers. This includes maintaining anti-
corruption efforts as a national priority, even in the face of political changes or 
competing policy agendas. A lack of political will could undermine the implementation 
of key interventions and slow progress towards the strategys goals. 
 Assumption of institutional capacity: The NACS assumes that public institutions,

5.4 Preliminary Risk Assessment: Resistance to the New M&E System 
One of the major risks identified in the Inception Report is the potential resistance from public 
institutions to the implementation of the new Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) system. This 
resistance may arise from concerns about increased oversight, fear of being held accountable 
for past or ongoing corruption, or institutional inertia. To assess this risk, it is important to 
consider the positions of various stakeholders within the anti-corruption ecosystem: 
 Government Agencies: While some government agencies, such as the Department of 
Planning, Monitoring, and Evaluation (DPME) and the National Anti-Corruption 
Advisory Council (NACAC), have shown a strong commitment to transparency and 
accountability, others with weaker governance structures may be more resistant to 
adopting the new M&E system. These agencies may perceive the system as a threat 
to their autonomy or fear that it could expose past failures in addressing corruption.

6.2 Analysis of annual performance plans 
At the NACS M&E Activities Alignment Workshop convened by GIZ on 14 May 2024, CLEAR-
AA was requested to conduct a review of Annual Performance Plans (APPs). The objective of 
the review was to identify what anti-corruption actions or measures departments undertook 
to implement and the indicators used by departments to monitor and report on progress. The 
review of the APPs forms part of the Landscape Analysis CLEAR-AA is required to deliver. This 
report presents the results of the review of APPS and will be incorporated into the Landscape 
Analysis Report. 
The review sought to answer the following key questions: 
1. To what extent do APPs reflect the measures identified in the NACS 
2. What outputs and indicators are used in the APPs to monitor implementation of anti-
corruption measures 
3. Is the APP a viable entry point for monitoring the implementation of the NACS in the public 
sector? 
The review analysed the 2024/25 Annual Performance Plans of 38 national 
departments/entities. This entailed mapping of outcomes, outputs and indicators relevant to 
anti-corruption. The mapping included scanning ministerial and accounting officers 
introductory statements, the situational analysis and Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, 
and Threats (SWOT), and the updated risk tables in the APP. The review used key words to 
identify anti-corruption measures, for example: accountability, conflict of interest, 
consequence management, corruption, disciplinary action, ethics, fraud, governance, 
integrity, procurement, transparency, whistleblowing.  
The results of the analysis are shown in Annex B. The main observations from the analysis are: 
1. The majority of APPs reviewed (28 out of 38) contained actions to address fraud and 
corruption, an indication that departments are paying attention to anti-corruption in their 
organisations. However, only 19 of the 38 APPs reviewed contained anti-corruption outputs 
and indicators to monitor implementation. Nine APPs identified anti-corruption measures in 
their updated risk logs, and in 10 APPs there was no mention of anti-corruption measures. 
2.  Departments in the Justice, Crime Prevention and Security Cluster were more likely to 
have outputs and indicators on anti-corruption than departments in other clusters. This is 
not surprising as these departments have mandates to enforce, investigate and prosecute.  
3. Departments in the Governance, State Capacity and Institutional Development Cluster anti-
corruption measures focused on monitoring the implementation of anti-corruption 
measures in the public sector. This is consistent with the oversight mandates of these 
departments. 
4. The review found that most departments included anti-corruption outputs and indicators 
under Programme 1: Administration. These outputs related to internal departmental anti-
corruption measures, for example, disciplinary action and consequence management.  
5. Anti-corruption measures were also found in the updated risk log contained in APPs. The 
risk mitigation strategies varied from broad statements, for example, implementing a fraud 
and corruption strategy to more specific measures, for example, Active collaboration with 
anti-corruption agencies.

7.3 Good practices and lessons from cross-country comparisons 
The anti-corruption experiences of other countries provide valuable lessons for the design and 
implementation of South Africa's National Anti-Corruption Strategy (NACS) Monitoring and 
Evaluation (M&E) system. By examining successful practices in Brazil, Indonesia, and other 
nations, the following practices and lessons have been identified as relevant to South Africa: 
 Brazil's Transparency Portal: Brazil has implemented a highly successful digital platform 
that allows citizens to access real-time information on government spending, 
procurement processes, and contract awards. This level of transparency has been crucial 
in holding public officials accountable and preventing corruption. The establishment of 
Brazil's Transparency Portal has shown that open data and public access to government 
information are critical tools in the fight against corruption. For South Africa, the lesson is 
clear: a similar online platform, where citizens can access information on public contracts, 
procurement processes, and financial disclosures, would significantly enhance 
transparency and accountability in government operations. 
 Indonesia's Bureaucratic Reform Index: Indonesias Bureaucratic Reform Index and the 
Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK) provide robust tools for monitoring public 
sector reforms and evaluating the impact of anti-corruption initiatives. These tools can 
help track institutional compliance with anti-corruption regulations and measure the 
effectiveness of enforcement actions. The Bureaucratic Reform Index underscores the 
importance of evaluating the effectiveness of institutions in implementing anti-corruption 
measures. South Africa can learn from this approach by integrating performance 
indicators into the NACS M&E system that measure institutional compliance with anti-
corruption policies and adherence to governance standards.  
 South Korea's Citizen Feedback Mechanism: South Koreas Anti-Corruption and Civil 
Rights Commission (ACRC) has integrated a real-time citizen feedback mechanism into its 
M&E system, allowing the public to report instances of corruption and providing a direct 
line for citizen participation. South Koreas success in incorporating citizen feedback 
mechanisms into its anti-corruption efforts highlights the importance of involving the 
public in monitoring and reporting corruption. By adopting similar mechanisms, South 
Africa can ensure that the NACS M&E system is responsive to the concerns of citizens and 
that public participation is central to anti-corruption efforts. 
 Ghanas institutional performance indicators and independent evaluation of its national 
anti-corruption plan: Ghana has performance indicators for monitoring the effectiveness 
of its key anti-corruption institutions. This is done to some extent in the South African 
context through the reporting against the Annual Performance Plans of departments. 
However, communication of institutional performance to the public is an area that could 
be enhanced. A comprehensive, independent evaluation of the NACS – the outcomes it 
has achieved, and its likely impact should also be on the NACAC and Government agenda.

8 Reflections on the NACS M&E System and strategic 
implications 
8.1 Key reflections 
The landscape analysis has highlighted both strengths and gaps within the NACS Monitoring 
and Evaluation (M&E) system. While the NACS M&E framework provides a comprehensive 
approach that considers the wider anti-corruption ecosystem, several challenges and realities 
must be taken into account to enhance its effectiveness. 
1. Incremental approach vs. comprehensive M&E System: Although the ambition is to 
establish a comprehensive M&E system that covers the entire anti-corruption ecosystem, 
the current institutional capacity and political realities suggest the need for an 
incremental approach. This gradual implementation strategy should prioritize 
strengthening core components of the M&E system while allowing room for scaling up as 
institutional capacity and stakeholder buy-in increase. 
2. Governance and integration challenges: A key risk identified in the analysis is the lack of 
coherence and integrated governance across the public sector, which undermines efforts 
to build a unified M&E system. The proliferation of anti-corruption strategies, 
frameworks, and interventions across different government departments and agencies 
has led to overlapping mandates and competing priorities. Addressing this fragmentation 
will be essential to creating a more streamlined and effective M&E system. 
3. Political and institutional context: The current political context and the ongoing 
transition to a Government of National Unity (GNU) present both opportunities and risks 
for the NACS. While the inclusion of diverse political voices can broaden support for anti-
corruption efforts, fluctuating political will and competing agendas pose a risk to 
sustained implementation. Ensuring that the NACS M&E system is resilient to political 
changes will be critical for its long-term success. 
4. Planning, budgeting, and reporting cycles: The realities of South Africas planning, 
budgeting, and reporting cycles also pose challenges for the effective integration of the 
NACS. Misalignment between the NACS and these cycles can result in delays and 
inconsistencies in implementation and reporting. A key recommendation is to synchronize 
the NACS M&E framework with existing governmental cycles to ensure that anti-
corruption targets are embedded into Annual Performance Plans (APPs) and other 
strategic planning documents. 
5. Stakeholder mapping and strategic opportunities: The stakeholder mapping exercise 
revealed a diverse array of actors involved in anti-corruption efforts, each with unique 
roles, interests, and capacities. This diversity presents both opportunities and challenges 
for building a cohesive M&E system. While the involvement of civil society and 
international partners offers valuable support, the risk of misalignment and divergent 
priorities must be managed. Clear roles and responsibilities need to be established to 
ensure that stakeholder engagement is effective and contributes to the overarching goals 
of the NACS. 
By taking these reflections into account, the NACS M&E system can evolve into a robust tool 
for guiding South Africas anti-corruption efforts. A focused, incremental approach that builds 
on existing strengths while addressing identified weaknesses will be essential for achieving 
meaningful and sustained impact.

8.2 Proposals for strengthening the implementation of the National Anti-
Corruption Strategy (NACS) 
8.2.1 Strengthening the Governance and Oversight Function of the GISCD Cluster 
 Role of the GISCD Cluster: The Governance, State Capacity, and Institutional 
Development (GISCD) Cluster plays a critical role in overseeing the implementation of 
anti-corruption measures within the public sector, including the monitoring of public 
service frameworks and systems. The cluster is responsible for ensuring that ethical 
governance practices are maintained across government departments and institutions. 
 Proposed Measures: To enhance its oversight capabilities, the GISCD Cluster should 
establish a dedicated anti-corruption task team that reports regularly on the 
implementation of NACS objectives. This task team could work closely with the National 
Anti-Corruption Advisory Council (NACAC) and other oversight bodies such as the Public 
Service Commission (PSC) to ensure coordination and accountability. 
 Benefits: Strengthening the GISCD Clusters oversight would improve monitoring and 
reporting mechanisms, ensuring timely interventions and preventing potential lapses in 
anti-corruption measures. It would also provide a more structured approach to 
institutionalizing anti-corruption policies across the public sector. 
8.2.2 Improved Communication on the Corruption Reform Programme 
 Importance of communication: Public confidence in the states ability to combat 
corruption is essential for the success of the NACS. An effective communication strategy 
will inform the public about the progress of anti-corruption reforms and enhance 
transparency, fostering trust in government institutions. 
 Communication strategies: The Government Communication and Information System 
(GCIS) should launch regular public updates through various media channels, including 
radio, television, social media, and government websites. These updates should highlight 
key milestones, successful prosecutions, and ongoing reforms. Additionally, creating a 
dedicated NACS website with progress dashboards and public reporting would enhance 
access to information. 
 Expected outcomes: Improved communication will build public trust and increase 
engagement in anti-corruption efforts. It will also support whistleblowers by promoting 
awareness of the protection mechanisms available to them. 
8.2.3 Institutionalizing NACS Interventions in the Medium-Term Development Plan 
(MTDP) and Annual Performance Plans (APPs) 
 Rationale: Embedding NACS objectives into the MTDP 2024-2029 and departmental APPs 
will ensure that anti-corruption interventions receive appropriate resources and attention 
in the public sector. These plans are critical for ensuring alignment between national 
development goals and anti-corruption efforts. 
 Implementation steps: The Department of Planning, Monitoring, and Evaluation (DPME) 
should work closely with all government departments to include NACS indicators in their 
APPs. These indicators should focus on areas like consequence management, 
procurement transparency, and whistleblower protection.

8.2.4 Strengthening Coordination Mechanisms for NACS Implementation 
 Rationale: Effective coordination is critical for ensuring the successful implementation of

Annex B: Review of Annual Performance Plans 2024/25 
Background 
Introduction 
At the NACS M&E Activities Alignment Workshop convened by GIZ on 14 May 2024, CLEAR-
AA was requested to conduct a review of Annual Performance Plans (APPs). The objective of 
the review was to identify what anti-corruption actions or measures departments undertook 
to implement and the indicators used by departments to monitor and report on progress. The 
review of the APPs forms part of the Landscape Analysis CLEAR-AA is required to deliver. This 
report presents the results of the review of APPS and will be incorporated into the Landscape 
Analysis Report. 
The review sought to answer the following key questions: 
4. To what extent do APPs reflect the measures identified in the NACS 
5. What outputs and indicators are used in the APPs to monitor implementation of anti-
corruption measures 
6. Is the APP a viable entry point for monitoring the implementation of the NACS in the public 
sector? 
 
Methodology  
The review analysed the 2024/25 Annual Performance Plans of national departments. This 
entailed mapping of outcomes, outputs and indicators relevant to anti-corruption. The 
mapping included scanning ministerial and accounting officers introductory statements, the 
situational analysis and SWOT, and the updated risk tables in the APP. The review used key 
words to identify anti-corruption measures, for example: accountability, conflict of interest, 
consequence management, corruption, disciplinary action, ethics, fraud, governance, 
integrity, procurement, transparency, whistleblowing. 
The review was not intended to be exhaustive, but rather a rapid analysis of the APPs. It was 
therefore confined to the APPs of national departments. It should be noted that these APPs 
were drafted prior to the existence of the Government of National Unity, which assumed 
office following the national election in May 2024. CLEAR-AA understands that the APPs may 
be revised once the Government of the 7th Administration approves the Medium-Term 
Development Plan 2024-2029.

It was beyond the scope of the review to assess the quality or relevance of the indicators used 
in the APPs. The review assumes that the quality of these indicators has already been assessed 
by DPME as part of the process of reviewing Strategic Plans and Annual Performance Plans. 
The APPs contain technical indicator descriptions, which provide important details including 
the targets, calculation, means of verification, reporting frequency and the sources of data. 
These can serve as a reference for the development of the NACS Indicator Framework. 
Review question 3. APPs as entry point for monitoring NACS implementation 
Observation 8. Even though only two APPs mentioned the NACS, APPs are nevertheless 
good entry points for monitoring the implementation of the NACS across the public service.

At the national level, at least half of the APPs include anti-corruption outputs and indicators 
and are in fact implementing aspects of the NACS even though they do not mention the NACS 
explicitly. The APPs are therefore an existing entry point for monitoring implementation, at 
least among national departments. Programme 1: Administration can be used to 
institutionalise the NACS as a transversal priority. This has been done for other priorities, for 
example, gender equality, gender-based violence, youth and disability.  
The review did not include provincial APPs. As the APPS of provincial departments follow the 
same structure as APPs of national departments, they too can serve as entry points for 
monitoring the implementation of the NACS at provincial level.  
Monitoring the implementation of the NACS at the level of local government falls within the 
mandate of the Department of Cooperative Governance. As the DCOG APP was not available 
for the review, CLEAR-AA is not able to provide any insights on monitoring of local government 
anti-corruption. 
Conclusion 
This rapid review of 38 Annual Performance Plans has generated useful insights into the 
potential value of APPs for institutionalising the NACS in the public sector and monitoring 
implementation of the NACS.  
The review confirmed that at least 50 percent of national departments are implementing anti-
corruption measures and have the intention to monitor and report on the implementation of 
these measures. The 2024/25 APPs contain potentially useful information for the 
development of the NACS Indicator Framework. The APPs may be revised to align with the 
forthcoming Medium-Term Development Plan. CLEAR-AA will revisit the APPs to ascertain 
whether amendments were made in respect of anti-corruption measures.
